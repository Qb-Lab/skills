# Languages and markets

The context's Constraints section names the geography and languages. That line decides;
nothing here assumes a country. If it is `INPUT_NEEDED`, write in the language of the
product's own copy and say that the market is unconfirmed.

## The rule for every language

Write each language version from the idea and the evidence, as a native speaker of that
audience would, not from the other language's text. A translated post keeps the first
language's rhythm, idioms and jokes, and reads as foreign. The versions may differ in hook,
length, examples and call to action; they must agree on facts.

- Keep product names, feature names and technical terms the way the product's own
  localisation writes them. Check the locale files before inventing a translation.
- Idioms, wordplay and cultural references are replaced with ones that work locally or
  dropped. Never translated literally.
- Humour, formality and directness are market-specific. When unsure, go one step plainer.
- Dates, currency, number formats and units follow the market.
- Hashtags and keywords are researched per language (what people in that market actually
  type), not translated.
- Banned-phrase thinking applies everywhere: every language has its own stock marketing
  phrases. Avoid the local equivalents of the list in `quality-bar.md`.
- If you cannot judge the naturalness of a language well enough to publish in it, say so
  and mark the draft `needs native review`. That is a normal status, not a failure.

## Arabic

- **Choose the register from the audience, and state the choice.** Modern Standard Arabic
  for formal, pan-regional or B2B writing. A dialect for social and conversational content
  when the audience is concentrated: Gulf (Khaleeji) for the UAE, Saudi Arabia and
  neighbours; Egyptian; Levantine; Maghrebi. Many brands use "white" Arabic, a simplified
  standard that most readers find natural. If the context does not say, ask once and record
  the answer in the context.
- **Do not mix dialects** in one piece, and do not write a dialect you would be
  approximating. Use plain standard Arabic and flag it for native review instead.
- **Write Arabic first for Arabic posts.** Sentence order, emphasis and connective words
  differ from English; a structure that is punchy in English can read as abrupt or childish.
  Arabic marketing copy often runs slightly warmer and more complete than its English
  counterpart, and that is correct, not padding.
- **English terms.** Tech and product words are frequently kept in English in Gulf social
  content. Follow the product's own Arabic UI and how the audience writes in the language
  bank in `<state>/audience.md`.
- **Numerals and punctuation.** Use whichever numerals the product's Arabic UI uses; if it
  shows none, use Western digits (the common choice in Gulf product interfaces) and note
  the assumption. Arabic punctuation (، ؛ ؟) in Arabic text.
- **Layout is right to left.** In visuals and video: text aligns right, reading order and
  motion direction flip, on-screen text needs an Arabic-capable typeface from the brand's
  fonts, and mixed Arabic and English lines must be checked for broken ordering. Screenshots
  come from the Arabic interface, not a mirrored English one.
- **Cultural fit.** Religious references, imagery of people, humour and occasions (Ramadan,
  Eid, national days) follow the market and the brand's own tone. When a campaign leans on
  one of these, have a person from the market review it.

## Bilingual accounts

The owner decides once per channel (ask once, then record it in the context): separate
accounts per language, alternate posts, or both languages in one post (rarely the best
choice outside announcements). A bilingual calendar plans each language's items separately
rather than
duplicating every slot.

## Other languages

The same rules apply. Add market notes for a new language to the project's context under
Constraints as they are learned, so they persist.
