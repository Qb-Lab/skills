# Visual realism

The test, applied to the concept before anything is generated and to the result afterwards:

> Would someone scrolling past immediately say "this is an AI image"?

If yes, the concept is wrong. Rewording the prompt does not fix a concept that only exists
because a model can render it. Go back to the brief and choose a scene a person with a
camera could have found.

## Clichés to design out

Avoid all of these unless the owner asked for one on purpose, and then say so in the brief.

| Cliché | Do this instead |
| --- | --- |
| Neon glow, purple or blue-to-pink gradients | the brand's actual colors as small accents in a real scene |
| Floating interface panels, screens hovering at angles | one real screenshot, flat, cropped to the point |
| Cyberpunk or sci-fi treatment of an ordinary product | the place the product is actually used |
| Holograms, particles, light trails, glowing lines | nothing; remove the effect and see if the idea survives |
| Plastic skin, perfect teeth, symmetrical faces | real people; or no face (hands, back of head, over the shoulder) |
| Creamy bokeh behind everything | phone-like depth: most of the frame in focus |
| Orange and teal "cinematic" grade on a basic scene | neutral white balance with the light the room would have |
| A laptop or phone in frame for no reason | a device only when the product lives on it, showing a real screen |
| Perfect symmetry, centred subject, tidy surfaces | off-centre framing, one thing cut by the edge, signs of use |
| Garbled or pseudo text on signs, screens, packaging | no generated text at all; real typography added afterwards |
| Too many fingers, fused hands, impossible grips | hands doing one simple thing, or out of frame |
| Stock-photo polish: team laughing at a screen | one person mid-task, not aware of the camera |
| Glass-walled offices with skyline views | the desk, kitchen, shop or van the audience really works from |
| "AI brain", circuit heads, robot hands, glowing nodes | show the output the product produces, not a metaphor for AI |

## What real images have

- **A light source you can point to.** A window, a ceiling strip, a phone flash. Shadows fall
  one way. Mixed colour temperature is normal indoors.
- **Evidence of life.** Cables, crumbs, a jacket on the chair, a scratched table, a half-read
  notification. Remove clutter and the image starts to look rendered.
- **A camera that was somewhere.** Eye height, arm's length, propped on a shelf. Name the
  position; "floating at a flattering angle" is a tell.
- **Imperfect timing.** Mid-blink, mid-gesture, slightly soft. Posed stillness reads as fake.
- **True scale and geography.** Sockets, signage, housing, food and clothing match the
  audience's region in the context. A generic "global" room belongs to no one.
- **Capture artefacts that match the device.** Phone HDR flatness and slight oversharpening
  for UGC; grain and gentle falloff for editorial. Not both.

## Hard rules

1. **Screenshots over fabrication.** If the product has an interface and screenshots exist
   or can be taken, the interface in the image is the real one. A generated mock of the
   product misrepresents it.
2. **Text is typeset, not generated.** Headlines, labels, prices and any non-Latin script are
   added afterwards with the brand's real typeface. Leave negative space for them.
3. **No generated person as a real one.** Never caption or imply that a generated figure is a
   customer, user, employee or the founder. Testimonial visuals use the real person's photo
   with permission, or no photo.
4. **No real people or brands without the right.** No celebrity likenesses, no competitor
   logos or packaging, no other creator's image or style lifted as-is.
5. **Product truth.** A physical product is shown as it is: real colour, real size, real
   features. Generation may build the scene, never improve the product.
6. **Disclose where required.** If the platform or the context's constraints require
   labelling synthetic media, the handoff says so.

## Checking a result

Look at the generated image at phone size first, then zoomed in. Reject and revise if any of
these is true: you can name the cliché; hands, teeth, eyes or text are wrong; the light has
no source; every surface is clean; the product or screen is not the real one; the frame
would work unchanged for a competitor. Two regeneration rounds at most; after that the
concept goes back to the brief or the route changes to a shoot.
